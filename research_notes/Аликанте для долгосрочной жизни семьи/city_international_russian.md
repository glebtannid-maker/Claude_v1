## 1. City or resort: size, growth, demographics, seasonality and year-round anchors

### Takeaway
Alicante is a real, fast-growing regional city, not a resort:
- 366,221 official residents (1 Jan 2025), up about 36,000 in ten years, in a metro area of about 0.87 M.
- Year-round anchors: about 25,000 university students, about 1,200 EU staff at EUIPO plus a European School of about 1,100 pupils, a tertiary hospital of about 3,500 staff, and a port-based tech hub.
- Cruise tourism (0.25–0.33 M passengers a year) is small compared with the resident population.

I could not obtain city-level age-structure or off-season data.

### Inferences
- **Clearly a city, not a resort.**
  - The population is growing about 2–3% a year, driven by working-age arrivals.
  - It is a provincial capital with a university of about 25,000 students, an EU agency, a large public hospital and a tech hub. None of these close off-season.
  - This contrasts with Torrevieja and Benidorm, where foreigners and retirees dominate: Torrevieja is 57% foreign against about 21% in the city (see section 4).
- **Cruise pressure is modest.** About 0.25–0.33 M cruise passengers a year is under one per resident per year. The record day (11,500) adds about 3% to the population for a few hours: noticeable on the Explanada, at the Castle and around the old town and markets, but not resort-scale.
- **Probably a younger city than the province.** About 8,000 new university students arrive each year, and growth comes mainly from working-age immigrants. So the city is probably noticeably younger than the province's 20.5% aged 65+, but no city age pyramid was obtained to confirm this.
- **The international professional niche is real but small.** EUIPO (about 1,200 staff from 27 EU countries, plus families) and the European School (about 1,100 pupils, with an English section) form an English- and multilingual-speaking middle-class niche that is unusual for a Spanish city of 366,000. That is exactly the milieu the family wants, but it is small: roughly 1% of the city's population.

### Inferences
- **Fine dining is mostly a weekend-trip resource.** The city has 1 star, but 12 more starred restaurants are elsewhere in the province (Elche, Marina Alta and Marina Baixa). For a well-off couple this suits weekend trips well; daily dining in the city is solid mid-to-upper range rather than a top gastronomic capital.
- **Coffee culture is adequate, not deep.** There is a specialty-coffee and brunch scene with several roasteries and cafés, enough for a couple used to Bali café culture. Its scale looks smaller than Valencia's or Lisbon's, but no counts exist to prove this.
- **Boredom risk:** no direct evidence could be gathered (see Gaps). The structural signals from section 1 point to an active year-round city of about 366,000, with a young student population and an international EU niche:
  - growing population
  - 25,000 students
  - 21% foreign residents
  - EUIPO

  But it is clearly smaller than Valencia or Málaga. Whether a 24-year-old couple gets bored in 1–2 years is likely to depend on using weekend trips (Valencia, Madrid, the mountains, the coast), which could not be quantified here.

## 3. City-level safety (Ministerio del Interior, Balance de Criminalidad)

### Takeaway
On the official 2025 data, Alicante city had about **36 recorded criminal offences per 1,000 inhabitants** (about 30 excluding cybercrime). That is roughly a quarter below Spain's average of about 50 (about 40.6 excluding cybercrime). Theft and online fraud dominate. The trend is rising, though: +12.2% in 2025 and about +7.8% in the first half of 2026.

## 4. How international is Alicante? Foreign residents, nationalities, where they live

About **1 in 5 residents of Alicante city is a foreign national**: 76,685 people, about 21%, at 1 Jan 2026 (provisional). In the province the share is 25.7%, the highest in Spain. But the city's foreigners are mostly Latin Americans (Spanish-speaking), North Africans (Algerians, Moroccans), Italians, Ukrainians and Russians. The northern-European "lifestyle" expats mainly live in the coastal towns. So the city is diverse, but it is not an English-speaking expat hub.

## 7. Russian-speaking services, avoiding a "Russian bubble", and attitudes toward Russians

### Takeaway
Russian-language infrastructure exists on the Costa Blanca: shops, churches, schools and doctors, according to a commercial source. It is concentrated in Torrevieja and the Marina Baixa, and 2022 reporting described Russians and Ukrainians in Alicante as coexisting peacefully. With Russians under about 1% of the city's population, living in Alicante city without a Russian bubble looks easy. However, specific services, chats, schools and churches in the city, and 2025–26 attitude or discrimination evidence, could not be researched.

### Cited Findings
